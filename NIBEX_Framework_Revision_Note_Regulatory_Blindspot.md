# Note 1 — The regulatory blind spot, forward-looking risk, and the free enterprise tension

**Origin:** surfaced while building the Companies House SIC-code hint for the complexity indicator's "regulated sector" question. Logged here for the full framework revision pass rather than resolved as a quick fix — this touches methodology, liability, and strategic scoring, not just one UI element.

---

## 1. The core problem

The "regulated sector" question, and Dimension 11.6 (Regulatory and Sector Compliance) more broadly, currently ask a backward-looking, factual question: *does a formal licence or regulatory requirement exist, and is it met?*

This misses activity that is genuinely high-risk but not yet formally regulated — the gap between something new coming to market and regulation catching up to it. A business could score well on both the complexity indicator and 11.6 while doing something with real potential for harm, simply because no regulator has caught up yet. Rules-based assessment cannot see this by construction, no matter how well-maintained the underlying data is.

## 2. Rules-based vs. judgement-based — an extension of an existing principle, not a new one

The Sutherland dual-path principle already establishes that judgement-based reasoning is as valid as data-based reasoning *within a given question* — the disqualifying factor is absence of reasoning, not absence of data. What this surfaces is a step earlier than that: the *questions themselves* may be framed in purely rules-based terms where a judgement-based framing is what's actually needed.

Proposed reframe for the regulated-sector question: not *"is this licensed/regulated"* but *"does this carry meaningful potential for harm — financial, physical, or legal — to customers or clients, whether or not it is currently subject to formal regulation."* This puts the assessor's reasoning at the centre and makes any data source (SIC code, a maintained sector list) a supporting input rather than the determination itself.

## 3. The strategic dimension — this is where it gets genuinely open

Raised directly in discussion: this isn't only about a business's current activity, but its stated direction. A business that communicates an intent to move into ambiguous or emergent-risk territory over the next 5–10 years is making a strategic choice with the same risk profile as if it were already operating there — arguably relevant to Dimension 10 (Strategic Clarity and Leadership), specifically 10.2 (Strategy and Planning) and 10.5 (Risk Awareness and Management), not just 11.6.

**The tension, stated plainly:** if NIBEX starts scoring down a business simply for *stating ambition* toward an uncertain-risk area, the framework risks becoming an instrument that discourages legitimate enterprise and innovation — punishing forward-thinking businesses for naming a direction, rather than rewarding businesses that plan for it responsibly. That would work against the framework's own credibility and against the kind of ambitious, growth-minded client NIBEX is built to serve.

**A possible resolution, not yet settled:** don't score the *ambition* itself. Score the *quality of risk reasoning* attached to it. A business moving toward an ambiguous-regulation space with no acknowledgement of the risk, no mitigation thinking, and no monitoring plan is a materially different (and worse) position than a business moving toward the same space with named risks, a stated mitigation approach, and an intent to track regulatory developments as they emerge. This mirrors the dual-path principle again — the disqualifying factor would be absence of demonstrated reasoning about the risk, not the presence of the risk or the ambition itself.

This would mean 10.2, 10.5, and 11.6 need to work together deliberately on this point rather than being scored in isolation — worth mapping out explicitly during the revision pass rather than assuming they already interact correctly.

## 4. Liability implications — needs honest treatment, not just methodology

Asking an assessor to judge "does this carry potential for harm" is a materially different, higher-stakes act than checking "does a licence exist." It's an expert risk call, not a fact-check. If Nicomachea's assessment records "no material concern" about something that later causes real harm, the exposure is different in kind from "the paperwork was in order at the time." This connects directly to the existing Simply Business conversation about how assessment work is classified for insurance purposes, and may need explicit scope-limiting language in client-facing documents (Services Agreement, MVP Participation Agreement) if this becomes part of the methodology — something to route past a solicitor alongside the other legal work already in motion, not decide unilaterally in the framework text.

## 5. Evidence standard implications

If "regulated" shifts from a factual check to a risk judgement, this may affect what counts as adequate evidence at Complete tier, where documented evidence is currently required for regulated sub-elements. Worth revisiting once the above is resolved — not urgent now, but a direct downstream consequence.

## 6. Why this can't be a one-time judgement — the Monitor connection

A business operating in a space that looks unregulated-but-fine today could look reckless in six months once regulation, public scrutiny, or a legal case catches up. This argues for treating "potential for harm" as something explicitly re-examined at each NIBEX Monitor cycle, not settled once and left. Worth building this into what a Monitor reassessment is specifically checking for, once the underlying question design is settled.

## 7. Open questions to resolve in the full revision pass

- Reword the regulated-sector complexity question and 11.6 criteria to ask about harm potential, not just formal regulatory status — exact wording still to be drafted
- Decide how 10.2, 10.5, and 11.6 should cross-reference or jointly inform scoring where a business names forward-looking strategic risk
- Resolve the free-enterprise tension explicitly: confirm the "score the reasoning, not the ambition" resolution, or find a better one
- Determine what, if any, disclaimer or scope-limiting language is needed in client-facing legal documents once this is live — solicitor input needed
- Revisit Complete-tier evidence standards for regulated/high-harm-potential sub-elements once question wording is settled
- Define what NIBEX Monitor should specifically re-check on this point at each reassessment cycle

# Note 2 — Is "people" a dimension, or the substrate everything else grows in?

**Origin:** raised in conversation with Ian Kinnery, who observed that businesses are fundamentally about people — started by people, advanced by people, existing to serve people. Worth taking seriously as a structural question about NIBEX's own architecture, not just a true-sounding aside.

## 1. The core problem

NIBEX currently gives "people" a home in two places: Dimension 7 (People, Capability, and Culture) and 10.6 (Owner Motivation and Energy — itself already flagged for rework, from a point-in-time emotional read to a resilience and sustainability assessment). Structurally, that treats people-related health as **two dimensions among eleven** — important, but a peer to things like cost structure or digital infrastructure, sitting in its own lane.

Ian's comment points at a different possibility: that people-related health isn't a peer dimension at all, but something closer to **the substrate the other ten grow in** — the thing that determines *how well* a business executes on economics, operations, and strategy, not just its own isolated corner of the assessment. A business can have a well-designed cost structure and still fail, because the people running it are burned out, misaligned, or working around each other rather than together.

## 2. Checking this against what's actually already there — the finding is more nuanced than "people is missing"

Going dimension by dimension, people-related influence already threads through more of NIBEX than first appears — it's just inconsistent, and mostly implicit rather than scored:

- **1. Economic Position** — no explicit people link, though the owner's own financial discipline and habits are frequently the real driver behind the numbers
- **2. Market Position** — the founder's own market understanding and network shape this heavily, unscored as such
- **3. Revenue Model** — 3.2 (Pricing Structure and *Confidence*) already explicitly names a psychological/people factor, arguably the closest thing NIBEX has to scoring this deliberately outside Dimension 7
- **4. Cost Structure** — little direct people link beyond general operational discipline
- **5. Stakeholders** — inherently about people and relationships; 5.2 (Key Person Dependency) is explicitly a people-risk sub-element
- **6. Operations** — 6.7 (Staff Communications and Rota Management) is explicitly people-facing; the rest is more systems-and-process
- **7. People, Capability, and Culture** — the dedicated home dimension
- **8. Customer Relationships** — inherently about people, but *external* people (customers), not the internal question Ian raised
- **9. Digital and Technology** — the least people-dependent dimension, mostly infrastructure
- **10. Strategic Clarity and Leadership** — explicitly leadership-driven; 10.6 is literally about the person running the business
- **11. Legal, Compliance, and Insurance** — 11.7 (Employment Law Compliance) is people-adjacent but procedural, not about people health itself

**The honest finding:** people-related factors aren't absent from the other nine dimensions — they're present, unevenly, and almost always **implicit and unscored** rather than named as a determining factor in the outcome. Dimension 1 doesn't ask whether the financial picture is actually a reflection of the owner's own relationship with money. Dimension 2 doesn't ask whether market understanding is genuinely institutional knowledge or one person's head, about to walk out the door.

## 3. The open design question — not yet resolved

Should NIBEX explicitly ask, within each dimension (or a defined subset of them), some version of: *"to what degree is this dimension's current state attributable to a people/leadership/culture factor, and how fragile or robust is that underlying factor?"* — as a deliberate cross-cutting thread, distinct from and additional to Dimension 7's own dedicated scoring.

**The genuine tension, worth naming rather than glossing over:** doing this well risks two failure modes in opposite directions —
- **Under-done**, it's a token box-tick that adds noise without real insight
- **Over-done**, it bloats every single sub-element with a duplicate "but how are the people" question, undermining the clean eleven-dimension structure that's part of NIBEX's own credibility and simplicity

**A structural option worth weighing**, mirroring how Note 1 resolved a similar tension: rather than a new question per dimension, a **narrative flag** — a place, within the existing Task List or Evidence Basis mechanism, for the assessor to note where a dimension's score is significantly people-dependent or people-fragile, without it becoming a formal numeric sub-question everywhere. This keeps the structure intact while still capturing the insight Ian's pointing at.

## 4. Why this matters beyond methodology purity

If people-fragility is real but currently invisible in the score, a business could score well overall while being one resignation or one burnout away from a very different picture — precisely the kind of thing a bank, investor, or acquirer (the audience Complete tier is designed to withstand scrutiny from) would want surfaced, not buried inside a Dimension 7 sub-score sitting quietly alongside ten others.

## 5. Open questions to resolve in the full revision pass

- Decide whether this becomes a structural addition (a cross-cutting question set) or a narrative flag mechanism, per the tension in Section 3
- If a narrative flag, define exactly where it lives (Task List? A new field? Evidence Basis?) and how it surfaces in the final report — this connects directly to the report-building work already done, since "strengths vs areas needing attention" grouping could plausibly need a "people-dependency" flag alongside it
- Revisit 10.6's planned rewrite (point-in-time emotional state → resilience and sustainability) with this broader question in mind, since the two are clearly related
- Decide which dimensions genuinely warrant this treatment and which don't — Section 2's dimension-by-dimension read is a starting point, not a final answer
- Consider whether this is itself a Monitor-cycle concern (people-fragility can change fast — a key departure, a burnout — in ways a single point-in-time assessment can't capture)

# Note 3 — Business Analyst techniques for strengthening NIBEX methodology

**Origin:** raised while comparing Product Management vs Business Analyst qualifications for David's own development. Six concrete BA techniques identified as directly applicable to NIBEX, independent of that career question — logged here as their own methodology improvement set.

## 1. Root cause analysis ("Five Whys")

NIBEX currently scores *what* is true (a sub-element sits at a 2) but doesn't formally trace *why*. A low score on 6.1 (Core Process Documentation) might genuinely trace back to 7.5 (Culture and Values) — nobody documents anything because the culture doesn't value it — but right now these sit as two disconnected numbers. **This is the concrete mechanism that could operationalise Note 2's "people as substrate" question** — rather than a vague cross-cutting flag, root cause analysis gives a structured way to actually trace a dimension's score back to an underlying people/culture driver where one exists.

## 2. Requirements traceability

Every Task List item should formally trace back to the *specific* scoring criterion that generated it, not float as a general recommendation. This directly strengthens the Complete tier's "withstand external scrutiny from banks, investors, acquirers" promise — a sceptical reader could trace any recommendation to its evidentiary root, rather than taking it on trust.

## 3. MoSCoW prioritisation (Must / Should / Could / Won't right now)

A cheap, high-value addition to the existing Task List feature — currently every task likely reads with equal weight. Applying MoSCoW would let a client see "fix this before anything else" clearly separated from "worth doing eventually."

## 4. As-is / To-be mapping

NIBEX's dimension scores are effectively "as-is" already, but there's no formal "to-be" statement per dimension. A short, explicit note per dimension — "what would a 5 actually look like for this specific business" — would make the gap concrete rather than leaving the client to infer it from the generic scoring scale description.

## 5. RACI mapping (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed)

Maps directly onto 5.2 (Key Person Dependency) and 7.2 (Role Definition and Clarity). Rather than just scoring "is this unclear," a RACI exercise during assessment would produce a genuinely useful standalone artefact for the client to keep.

## 6. Process mapping (lightweight BPMN-style)

Would give real teeth to 6.1 (Core Process Documentation) beyond asking "do documented processes exist" — a simple visual map of how a task actually moves through the business, who touches it, where it stalls, is more useful to a client than a bare numeric score.

**Priority, if picking where to start**

**Root cause analysis and requirements traceability first** — the former deepens the framework's intellectual rigor and connects directly to Note 2; the latter directly serves the Complete-tier credibility promise. Both are a discipline applied to what already exists, not a rebuild.

# Note 4 — Root cause analysis: the anchoring risk and the false correlation risk

**Origin:** raised the day after root cause analysis (Note 3, item 1) was actually built and shipped — a genuine second-order question about how that feature could go wrong in practice, not just whether it should exist.

## 1. The "doesn't know what's wrong" problem — don't auto-suggest conclusions

A tempting next step would be a feature that scans a completed assessment and proposes likely causal links ("Dimension 1 and 3.2 often correlate — consider linking them"). **This should not be built.** It introduces anchoring — a well-documented bias where, once a plausible hypothesis is shown to someone, they gravitate toward confirming it rather than genuinely testing it, even when they're skilled and acting in good faith. This directly undermines the Sutherland dual-path principle already at the heart of NIBEX: the disqualifying factor is absence of reasoning, not absence of data — and an accepted suggested correlation is absence of reasoning wearing a data-shaped costume.

**The resolution:** if a pattern exists, the system's role is to prompt the assessor to go back and *test* it with the client directly — not to pre-fill or suggest the link itself. This mirrors exactly how AI scoring already works: it suggests, but a human must actively review and accept, never silently applied.

## 2. The false correlation problem — investigation only helps if it's built to falsify, not confirm

Left unguided, "go investigate further" doesn't reliably catch a false correlation, because people default to confirmation-seeking — once a link is suspected, supporting evidence gets noticed and contrary evidence gets unconsciously discounted. This is normal human reasoning, not a flaw specific to any one assessor, which is exactly why the methodology needs to actively guard against it rather than assume good faith is enough.

**Concrete fix, ready to build now:** add a short field alongside the existing Root Cause dropdown (built in Note 3): *"What would prove this link is wrong?"* This forces the assessor to articulate a genuine falsification test, not just a supporting story. If they can't answer it, that's itself a signal the link is a guess rather than a genuine finding.

**A sharper trap within this, caught live during testing:** the falsification question can get answered with something like *"I don't have enough knowledge about identifying other revenue streams"* — which sounds like an honest, humble answer, but isn't actually a falsification test at all. It answers a different question — *"what don't I personally know"* — rather than the one asked — *"what fact about the business would prove this link wrong."* Left unchecked, this produces a specific, three-layer failure: (1) the original score may already rest on a misread of what was being asked, (2) the root cause link is itself a guess about causation, and (3) when asked to challenge that guess, the person reaches for self-doubt rather than actually interrogating the business claim. Each layer sounds like honesty in the moment, but stacked together they produce a confident-looking, fully-documented root cause link that was never genuinely tested — just quietly agreed with by default, three times over. **Fixed in the interface itself** by making the field's own text explicitly state the distinction: this should be a fact about the business, not a gap in the assessor's own knowledge, and "I'm not sure" means the link needs testing with the client, not that the link is wrong or that the assessor is at fault.

## 3. A related, future-only risk — not urgent, worth remembering

If NIBEX's data-asset ambition ever leads to scanning correlations **across many completed assessments** (rather than within a single one), a separate statistical problem appears: with eleven dimensions, there are 55 possible pairings, and purely by chance some pairs will appear correlated across a large enough dataset even with no real relationship at all — the same reason a long enough run of coin flips eventually throws up a streak that means nothing. Not a concern while assessment volume is low; worth flagging now so it isn't rediscovered as a surprise once the data asset actually has scale.

# Note 5 — Why AI shouldn't set task priority, even though it scores sub-elements

**Origin:** raised after building the end-of-assessment task review screen (MoSCoW prioritisation) — should AI be involved in suggesting or setting priority, the same way it assists with scoring suggestions?

**The answer: no, and for the same core reason as Note 4's root cause finding.** The anchoring risk — once a plausible suggestion is shown, people tend to accept it rather than genuinely test it, even in good faith — applies at least as strongly here, arguably more so. The review screen exists specifically so prioritisation happens as one considered pass, with the whole assessment visible at once. Pre-filling suggested priorities the moment that screen opens would quietly undo the exact thing it was built to protect: genuine, informed human weighing of what matters most.

**A second, distinct reason specific to this case:** priority is inherently relative and business-specific — "Must have" for one business may be "Could have" for another with different risk tolerance or circumstances. That judgement depends on context an AI reading only task text doesn't have access to — things like a client's cash position, relationships, or appetite for risk, discussed in conversation but never captured as assessment data.

**Where the line sits, consistent with how AI is already used elsewhere in NIBEX:** the existing AI scoring assist only ever suggests, and always requires an explicit human "Accept." If AI involvement in prioritisation is ever revisited, the only acceptable version would follow the same pattern — a subtle optional prompt, never a pre-filled or auto-applied priority. Not something to build now; the review screen's value is precisely that human judgement operates there without a machine's thumb on the scale.
